# 1. What "tipping the scales" means mechanically

The regime is a fee-shifting sanction with **one arithmetic trigger and one discretionary escape**. Everything a carrier could want falls into one of five levers:

| # | Lever | Current state | Direction of a defense-favorable change |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | **The 25% differential** — § 768.79(7) (2024) | Symmetric: defendant needs judgment ≥25% below its offer; claimant needs ≥25% above its demand | **Lower the defendant's threshold, raise the claimant's** |
| **2** | **Good faith** — § 768.79(8)(a) | The *only* discretionary escape from an otherwise mechanical entitlement, and the corpus's emptiest arc | **Convert discretion into a safe harbor** |
| **3** | **Validity traps** — chiefly rule 1.442(c)(3) apportionment | Kills defense proposals more often than claimant demands, because global multi-provider offers are the defense's normal instrument | **Neutralize apportionment substantively** |
| **4** | **The measure of the award** | Fees run from the date of the proposal | **Run them from the § 766.106 presuit notice** |
| **5** | **The consequence of rejection** | Fees and costs only | **Add a damages consequence** (Scenario C) |

Two structural facts about this regime make the levers usable, and both are established in the project corpus.

**First, entitlement is arithmetic, not equitable.** [*TGI Friday's, Inc. v. Dvorak*, 663 So. 2d 606 (Fla. 1995)](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1715682/tgi-fridays-inc-v-dvorak/) settled that once the numbers line up the court has no discretion to deny fees because rejection was reasonable. **The only way out is a good-faith finding.** So lever 2 is worth more than it looks: closing the good-faith escape makes every other lever self-executing.

**Second, the good-faith escape is doctrinally empty.** [`arc-matrix.md`](../arc-matrix.md) finding #2: arc 6 has five entries in thirty-nine merits cases and **four of them are asides** — *Leapai* n.2's 1992 doubt about "a one dollar offer," *Frosti*'s unchallenged $1 punitive proposal, *Anderson*'s note that motive goes to good faith rather than validity, and *Coates*'s reliance on the (8)(b) factors. **No decision in the read corpus squarely resolves a good-faith challenge.** That vacuum is currently filled by trial-court discretion, which in a medical negligence case with a sympathetic plaintiff runs against the carrier. Legislating into an empty arc is the cheapest thing on this list.

### Why medical malpractice specifically

Four features distinguish med-mal from the general tort population and each one supplies a findings-grade rationale:

1. **Unbounded noneconomic tail.** ⚠️ After *Estate of McCall v. United States*, 134 So. 3d 894 (Fla. 2014), and *North Broward Hospital District v. Kalitan*, 219 So. 3d 49 (Fla. 2017), § 766.118's noneconomic caps are gone. Florida is one of the few large states with no cap on medical noneconomic damages. Verdict variance is therefore extreme, which makes calibrating a proposal to beat a 25% differential close to guesswork.
2. **Chapter 766 front-loads the carrier's costs.** A defendant served with a § 766.106 notice of intent must investigate, obtain a corroborating expert opinion under § 766.203, and respond — all **before suit is filed**. Rule 1.442(b) then bars a proposal until **90 days after service of process**. The carrier knows the case's value on day one of the lawsuit and cannot shift a dollar of fees for three more months.
3. **Multi-provider defense.** The normal med-mal defense structure is physician + practice group + hospital + sometimes a staffing entity, frequently under one carrier or one defense program. The natural instrument is **one global proposal** — which is exactly the instrument rule 1.442(c)(3) invalidates.
4. **A live legislative bargain.** The 2025 veto of the "free kill" repeal, and the Governor's stated condition that he would sign a repeal only paired with limits on recovery, means there is **an actual trade on the table**. See Part 6.

⭐ **The strategic core of this memo:** the Legislature has twice tried to limit medical malpractice recovery through **damages caps**, and the Florida Supreme Court struck them both times. ⚠️ *McCall* and *Kalitan* did so on **equal protection** grounds, not access to courts. **§ 768.79 is the caps substitute.** It reaches the same economic result — reducing expected claim value and settlement leverage — through a mechanism the Court has never invalidated in forty years, and through classifications based on **litigation conduct** rather than **claimant identity**. That distinction is doing all the constitutional work in Parts 4 and 5.

# 2. The three gates every amendment must clear

## Gate 1 — Article V, § 2(a): substance or procedure

This is the gate that kills sloppy drafting, and it is the one Florida practitioners most often misjudge.

**The test** (from [*Massey v. David*, 979 So. 2d 931 (Fla. 2008)](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1786026/massey-v-david/), quoting *Haven Federal Savings & Loan v. Kirian*): substantive law "creates, defines, and regulates rights"; practice and procedure are "the course, form, manner, means, method, mode, order, process or steps by which a party enforces substantive rights."

**Applied to this field**, [*Knealing v. Puleo*, 675 So. 2d 593 (Fla. 1996)](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1725782/knealing-v-puleo/) draws the line cleanly:

- §§ 45.061 and 768.79 **survived** — each contains a substantive fee-authorizing provision.
- **§ 44.102(6) did not.** The Legislature tried to alter proposal *timing* through the mediation statute. Purely procedural, no substantive authorization, struck.

**The operational rule for drafting:**

| Safe — substantive | Fatal — procedural |
|---|---|
| Who is entitled to fees, and when | When a proposal may be served |
| The differential threshold | What a proposal must say, and in what form |
| What the award measures, and from what date | Service, filing, acceptance windows |
| Whether entitlement survives a defect | Whether a proposal is *valid* |
| Damages consequences of rejection | Anything rule 1.442 already covers |

🔴 **The single most important drafting instruction in this memo:** never write a provision that regulates *the content of a proposal*. Write provisions that regulate *entitlement*. Rule 1.442(c)(3) is a content rule; a statute that says "a joint proposal need not state an amount attributable to each party" is arguably a content rule too, and under [*Timmons v. Combs*, 608 So. 2d 1 (Fla. 1992)](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1842760/timmons-v-combs/) the rule supersedes conflicting procedural statutes. The same objective stated as *"entitlement under this section is not defeated by the failure of a proposal to state an amount attributable to each offeree"* is an entitlement rule and survives.

⭐ **And there is a second, sharper route at the same target.** [*Kuhajda v. Borden Dairy Co. of Alabama, LLC*, 202 So. 3d 391 (Fla. 2016)](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4314140/susanne-l-kuhajda-v-borden-dairy-company-of-alabama-llc/) holds that a rule provision gets strict enforcement **only where it implements a substantive requirement of the statute**; where it does not, "[t]he procedural rule should no more be allowed to trump the statute here than the tail should be allowed to wag the dog."

*Trace Elements* keeps (c)(3) alive precisely by holding that it **does** implement a substantive requirement — the singular "party" in § 768.79(2)(b), read that way in [*Allstate Indemnity Co. v. Hingson*, 808 So. 2d 197, 199 (Fla. 2002)](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1860954/allstate-indem-co-v-hingson/).

**So the Legislature can pull the foundation out from under the rule without touching the rule.** Amend § 768.79(2)(b) to codify what Harding, J., said in dissent — that § 1.01(1), Fla. Stat., already makes the singular include the plural — and (c)(3) is left implementing *nothing substantive*. Under *Kuhajda*'s own logic it then cannot defeat statutory entitlement. **The Legislature legislates on the statute; *Kuhajda* does the work on the rule.**

⚠️ Caveat worth stating plainly: this depends on *Trace Elements*'s reasoning surviving rehearing, and on the Court applying *Kuhajda* rather than distinguishing it. It is the most elegant move available and it is not a sure thing.

## Gate 2 — Article I, § 21: access to courts

"The courts shall be open to every person for redress of any injury."

⚠️ Under *Kluger v. White*, 281 So. 2d 1 (Fla. 1973), the Legislature may not abolish a right of redress that existed at common law or by statute as of 1968 without providing a **reasonable alternative** or showing an **overpowering public necessity** with no alternative means.

**Levers 1–4 do not implicate this gate meaningfully.** A fee-shifting consequence for declining a settlement offer does not abolish a cause of action. The claimant may still sue, still try the case, still recover.

**Lever 5 does.** A damages consequence — capping or eliminating noneconomic recovery for a claimant who rejects a qualifying offer — is a restriction on redress and will draw a *Kluger* challenge.

⭐ ⚠️ **And the answer to that challenge already exists in chapter 766.** *University of Miami v. Echarte*, 618 So. 2d 189 (Fla. 1993), upheld §§ 766.207 and 766.209 against exactly this attack. That scheme does precisely what Scenario C proposes: a claimant who **refuses** voluntary binding arbitration has noneconomic damages capped (§ 766.209(4), $350,000 per incident, economic losses limited to medicals plus 80% of wage loss); a claimant who **accepts** gets prompt payment, and where the *defendant* refuses, the claimant gets damages plus fees up to 25% of the award (§ 766.209(3)). *Echarte* held the "commensurate benefit" satisfied *Kluger*.

**That structure is thirty-three years old, still on the books, and has never been struck.** Any Scenario C bill should be drafted as its sibling and should say so on its face.

## Gate 3 — Equal protection, and the medical-malpractice-specific traps

**Rational basis governs.** Economic and remedial legislation. Under ordinary Florida rational-basis review, a med-mal-specific offer-of-judgment rule with contemporaneous legislative findings should hold.

**Four things to watch.**

**(a) ⚠️ The *McCall* lesson is about *findings*, not about caps.** The 2014 plurality's problem with § 766.118 was that the 2003 legislative findings of a "medical malpractice crisis" were no longer supported by current conditions, and that the cap's burden fell arbitrarily — a wrongful-death cap that shrank each survivor's share as the number of survivors grew. **Two takeaways, both actionable:**

- **Build a current record.** Findings must rest on 2026–2027 data, not on 2003 recitals. Florida already has the collection mechanism: the mandatory closed-claim reporting under § 627.912 and the Office of Insurance Regulation's annual medical malpractice financial reports. That is the evidentiary spine (Part 6).
- **Classify by conduct, not by status.** *McCall*'s fatal defect was a burden allocated by an accident of family composition. **A consequence triggered by a claimant's own decision to reject a documented offer is a conduct-based classification**, and rational-basis review is far more forgiving of those. This is the strongest single constitutional argument available to any of these scenarios and it should be stated in the findings.

**(b) 🔴 Article I, § 26 — the sleeper, and it exists only in medical malpractice.** ⚠️ The 2004 "Claimant's Right to Fair Compensation" amendment guarantees that in a medical liability claim involving a contingency fee, the claimant receives **no less than 70% of the first $250,000 in all damages, exclusive of reasonable and customary costs, and 90% above that**.

A fee award against a claimant who recovers something but fails to beat the proposal can drive net recovery below that floor. The counterarguments are real — § 26 by its terms constrains the **contingency fee agreement**, an adverse fee award is not the claimant's lawyer's fee, and the Court has permitted claimants to **waive** § 26's protection — but this is the one constitutional objection that has no analogue in property, auto, or general liability, and it is the objection a competent claimant's lawyer will lead with.

⭐ **Mitigation, and it does double duty.** Make a fee award against a claimant **non-recourse — collectible only out of the judgment — where the claimant obtains a judgment, and fully recourse where the judgment is one of no liability.** That:

- removes the § 26 argument in the only fact pattern where it bites (partial recovery);
- preserves the full deterrent in the fact pattern the carriers actually care about (the meritless case that goes to a defense verdict);
- and pre-empts the political attack — "this bill bankrupts widows" — at almost no cost, because on a defense verdict there is no recovery to protect.

**Give this up early and loudly. It is the cheapest concession in the package.**

**(c) Article III, § 11 special laws.** Not a serious obstacle — a general law classifying by type of action is not a special law. But do not name insurers as the beneficiary class in the operative text; classify by *action* ("an action for medical negligence governed by chapter 766"), not by *party*.

**(d) The precedent that makes all of this ordinary.** ⭐ **Ch. 2022-271 § 24 already put an industry-specific carve-out inside § 768.79.** Subsection (6) reads in its entirety: *"For a breach of contract action, a property insurer may make a joint offer of judgment or settlement that is conditioned on the mutual acceptance of all the joint offerees."* It has sat unchallenged for four years. **The Legislature has already established that § 768.79 can be tuned for one industry, in one category of action.** That is the template, and it is worth citing in the bill analysis.

# 3. Scenario A — The Technical Package

**Framing:** "Fixing three drafting traps that punish parties who try to settle."
**Ask:** narrow, technical, non-headline.
**Audience:** the House Civil Justice & Claims Subcommittee, which is where insurance-fee bills go to die.

## What it does

**A-1. Good-faith safe harbor.** Amend § 768.79(8)(a) so that, in a chapter 766 action, a proposal is **presumed made in good faith** where at the time of service the offeror held a written medical expert opinion under § 766.202(6) supporting its valuation, and served a written statement of the basis for the amount. Rebuttable only by clear and convincing evidence.

**A-2. Presuit fee accrual.** Amend the fee-measure provision so that, in a chapter 766 action, an offeror's recoverable fees and costs include those incurred **on or after service of the § 766.106 notice of intent**, rather than only from the date of the proposal.

**A-3. Entitlement preservation.** Add: entitlement under § 768.79 is **not defeated** by a proposal's failure to state an amount attributable to each offeree, where the total amount and all nonmonetary terms are stated with particularity. Paired with the conforming amendment to § 768.79(2)(b) codifying § 1.01(1).

## Why each clears the gates

| | Gate 1 (art. V) | Gate 2 (*Kluger*) | Gate 3 (EP / § 26) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A-1** | Good faith is **in the statute**, at (8)(a). Legislating its standard is legislating the statute's own substantive escape clause. Clean. | N/A | Rational basis, easy |
| **A-2** | Pure fee-measure. *Knealing* says the statute's substantive fee authorization is exactly what saves it. Clean. | N/A | Enlarges an award; no § 26 issue unless combined with B/C |
| **A-3** | 🔴 **The exposed one.** Must be written as entitlement, never as proposal content. See Gate 1. | N/A | N/A |

## Value to carriers

**A-1 is the highest-value-per-unit-of-risk item in this entire memo.** It converts the only discretionary escape from a mechanical entitlement into a near-automatic one, in the single arc where the Supreme Court has decided nothing. Trial courts currently deny defense fee awards on good-faith grounds after defense verdicts; a rebuttable presumption tied to an expert opinion the carrier already has under chapter 766 largely ends that.

**A-2 is worth real money and almost nothing politically.** In a med-mal case the presuit period is 90 days minimum and frequently longer with extensions; adding that to the fee-shifting window materially raises the cost of rejecting.

**A-3 is the *Trace Elements* fix.** Note that it is **facially side-neutral** — it helps whoever makes the joint proposal — but **practically defense-favorable**, because in medical negligence the multi-provider global proposal is the defense's standard instrument, while claimants in wrongful death proceed through a single personal representative under § 768.20.

⚠️ **Timing caution on A-3.** The *Trace Elements* majority **invited a rule amendment** and expressly cited the post-*Lamb* adoption of (c)(4) as its model. If the Civil Procedure Rules Committee delivers that amendment first, A-3's cover story ("we are just fixing a broken doctrine") passes to the Court, and A-3 becomes a redundant ask. **There is a window, and the rehearing outcome opens or closes it.**

## Odds

| | Assessment |
|---|---|
| **Passage, standalone, 2027 session** | **25–35%.** HB 1269 (2026), an attorney-fee bill in insurance claims, died in Civil Justice & Claims without a hearing. Fee bills do not move on their own merits. |
| **Passage, attached to a health-care or tort package** | **60–70%.** As a technical rider on a bill that already has leadership behind it, this is the kind of thing that survives conference. |
| **Surviving the Florida Supreme Court** | **A-1: 90%+. A-2: 90%+. A-3: 65%.** A-1 and A-2 are as clean as fee legislation gets. A-3 carries the art. V risk and depends on *Kuhajda* being applied rather than distinguished. |
| **Economic value to carriers** | Moderate. Meaningful improvement in fee recovery rates; no change to expected claim value. |

# 4. Scenario B — The Asymmetric Trigger

**Framing:** "Rebalancing a sanction that has stopped producing settlements."
**Ask:** visible, quantifiable, contested.
**Audience:** leadership. This does not pass out of a subcommittee on its own.

## What it does

Scenario A, plus a new subsection creating a chapter 766 entitlement structure:

- **Defendant's threshold falls to zero differential.** A defendant is entitled to fees where the judgment is one of no liability **or is less than the amount of the proposal** — no 25% margin.
- **Claimant's threshold rises to 50%.** A claimant is entitled only where the judgment obtained is at least 50% greater than the demand.
- **The § 26 mitigation** from Gate 3(b): non-recourse against a claimant who obtains a judgment; full recourse on a judgment of no liability.
- **Findings** built on OIR closed-claim and financial-report data.

## Why the asymmetry is defensible on the merits

It is not merely a thumb on the scale, and the bill analysis should say why:

1. **The differential exists to absorb valuation uncertainty, and the uncertainty is not symmetric.** A defendant must value a claim before knowing what a jury will do with noneconomic damages that Florida no longer caps. A claimant valuing its own demand is valuing its own claim. ⚠️ Post-*McCall*/*Kalitan*, the defense's variance is genuinely larger.
2. **The defendant already bears an asymmetric floor.** § 768.79 lets a defendant recover on a judgment of no liability, but a claimant who wins nothing simply loses; the 25% differential on top of that is a second cushion for the party that already gets the categorical one.
3. **The 2022 property-insurer carve-out is the working precedent** for calibrating this statute to one industry's litigation economics.

## Where it is vulnerable

**The equal protection challenge is real but survivable.** ⚠️ A challenger runs *McCall*: medical negligence claimants are singled out for worse treatment than other tort claimants without a current factual basis. Three answers:

- **Conduct, not status.** The burden attaches to a claimant's decision to reject a documented offer, not to who the claimant is or how many siblings they have. That is the precise defect *McCall* identified, and this bill does not have it.
- **Findings.** Contemporaneous, data-backed. This is the whole ballgame — see Part 6.
- **Bench composition.** ⚠️ *McCall*'s equal protection analysis was a **plurality**, and *Kalitan* extended it in 2017. Of the justices who produced those results, **only Labarga remains** on the August 2026 Court — Couriel (C.J. since July 1, 2026), Muñiz, Grosshans, Sasso, Francis, and Tanenbaum have all joined since. A modern majority for extending *McCall* to a fee-shifting statute is difficult to see.

**The art. V challenge is confined to the apportionment element** — the same A-3 exposure, mitigated the same way.

**The political attack is the real problem.** "The Legislature made it cheaper to injure patients and then billed the patients for the defense." The § 26 non-recourse mitigation blunts it. Nothing eliminates it.

## Odds

| | Assessment |
|---|---|
| **Passage, single session (2027)** | **30–40%.** This is HB 837-class legislation in a post-2025 environment where the "free kill" repeal has organized a survivor constituency that did not exist in 2023. It needs leadership, a governor, and a trade. |
| **Passage across two sessions (2027–2028)** | **50–60%.** Florida tort legislation habitually fails once and passes the second time in narrowed form. Expect the 50% claimant threshold to be the first thing cut. |
| **Surviving the Florida Supreme Court** | **65–75%**, and **75–85%** if the § 26 non-recourse mitigation and current findings are both in the bill. The exposure is concentrated in the apportionment element and in § 26, not in the thresholds. |
| **Economic value to carriers** | **High.** Changes expected claim value, not merely fee recovery. A zero-differential defense trigger makes every unaccepted proposal a live fee exposure and moves settlement postures across the whole book. |

# 5. Scenario C — The *Echarte* Play

**Framing:** "A voluntary early-resolution mechanism, modeled on the arbitration process this Court upheld in 1993."
**Ask:** the largest. This is the caps substitute.
**Audience:** the Governor's office first, the Legislature second.

## What it does

Do **not** rebuild § 768.79's mechanics. Create a chapter 766 sibling to §§ 766.207/.209 — call it a **qualified offer of resolution** — and amend § 768.79 only to yield to it.

**The offer.** A defendant may serve a qualified offer accompanied by (i) a written expert opinion under § 766.202(6), (ii) a stipulation to entry of judgment in the offered amount, and (iii) an irrevocable commitment to pay within 30 days of acceptance.

**If the claimant accepts:** judgment enters, payment within 30 days, plus the claimant's reasonable attorney's fees up to 25% of the award — **mirroring § 766.209(3)**, the provision that already exists and already survived. No trial risk, no delay, no appeal.

**If the claimant rejects and fails to obtain a judgment exceeding the offer:** noneconomic damages are limited to a statutory figure, and the defendant recovers fees and costs from the date of the offer — non-recourse per Gate 3(b).

**If the defendant makes no qualified offer:** nothing changes. The claimant's position is exactly what it is today.

## Why this is the strongest constitutional design of the three

⭐ **It is the same trade *Echarte* blessed, in the same chapter, with the same structure.**

| § 766.207 / .209 (upheld 1993) | Proposed § 766.2085 |
|---|---|
| Elective on both sides | Elective on both sides |
| Claimant who accepts: prompt resolution, fees up to 25% of award | Claimant who accepts: payment in 30 days, fees up to 25% of award |
| Claimant who refuses: noneconomic damages capped, economic damages limited | Claimant who refuses **and loses the comparison**: noneconomic damages limited |
| Defendant who refuses: claimant recovers damages plus fees | Defendant who makes no offer: nothing changes |
| ⚠️ Upheld against *Kluger* — "commensurate benefit" | Same benefit, and **conditioned on the claimant's own choice** |

⚠️ Two respects in which the proposal is **stronger** than the statute *Echarte* upheld:

1. The § 766.209(4) consequence attaches to a claimant who refuses arbitration **regardless of the merits**. Here the consequence attaches only where the claimant **rejected an offer and then failed to beat it** — a narrower and more obviously rational trigger.
2. ⚠️ *McCall*'s objection to § 766.118 was arbitrary allocation among survivors. Nothing here allocates by family composition.

## Why it is the biggest prize politically

⭐ **This is the only one of the three that gives a governor the "limits on recovery" that the May 2025 veto demanded.** It is a **defendant-elected, conduct-triggered cap** — which is exactly the form of limitation the Court has *not* struck, as opposed to the mandatory across-the-board form it struck twice.

That means Scenario C's political probability is not independent. It is **coupled to the largest bargaining chip in Florida health-care politics**: the "free kill" repeal, which passed both chambers in 2025, was vetoed, and died again in Senate Rules on March 13, 2026. Its sponsors will bring it back. Scenario C is the price.

## Where it is vulnerable

- ⚠️ **A challenger will argue *McCall* and *Kalitan* undermined *Echarte*'s cap reasoning.** They did not squarely — those were equal protection holdings about § 766.118, and *Echarte* was an access-to-courts holding about §§ 766.207/.209, which remain on the books. But the argument will be made and the Court has not addressed the interaction. **This is the single largest unquantified risk in the memo, and it should be researched before a bill is drafted.** ⚠️ *Franks v. Bowers*, 116 So. 3d 1240 (Fla. 2013), addressing private med-mal arbitration agreements against the chapter 766 scheme, is directly adjacent and unread.
- **Set the noneconomic figure carefully.** Too low and it reads as § 766.118 by another name and invites the Court to treat it as one. A figure indexed to inflation and set at or above the existing § 766.209(4) benchmark makes the "this is the arbitration scheme's sibling" argument much harder to dismiss.
- **Complexity.** A new mechanism requires new practice. Carriers will need two years to use it well.

## Odds

| | Assessment |
|---|---|
| **Passage, standalone** | **15–25%.** Too large to pass on its own merits. |
| **Passage as the paired condition on a "free kill" repeal** | **45–60%**, conditional on the January 2027 governor's posture. If the incoming governor holds the same position as the May 2025 veto, the trade is available and both sides have a reason to make it. |
| **Surviving the Florida Supreme Court** | **60–70%.** Higher than a mandatory cap by a wide margin because of the *Echarte* pedigree and the elective structure; lower than Scenarios A and B because it is the only one that restricts damages at all. |
| **Economic value to carriers** | **Highest.** A defendant-elected ceiling on noneconomic exposure changes reserving, reinsurance, and pricing — not just litigation outcomes. |

## 6.3 The data predicate — the thing most likely to be skipped and most likely to matter

⚠️ ***McCall*'s core lesson is that stale findings kill a statute.** The 2003 recitals of a medical malpractice crisis were the vulnerability. So:

1. **Pull the OIR closed-claim database.** Florida has mandatory medical malpractice closed-claim reporting under **§ 627.912**, plus the Office of Insurance Regulation's annual medical malpractice financial reports. This is a public, statutorily-created dataset built for exactly this purpose.
2. **Produce the metric that matters:** the distribution of med-mal outcomes relative to pre-trial offers. Specifically — what fraction of tried med-mal cases produce a verdict below the last defense offer, and what fraction of those produced a § 768.79 fee award? **If the first number is large and the second is small, that gap is the bill.** It is a finding about a statute failing at its own stated purpose, which is a far better frame than "premiums are high."
3. **Quantify the trap.** Count the med-mal fee awards lost to rule 1.442(c)(3) apportionment. ⚠️ This requires the district-court corpus, which [`memo.md`](../memo.md) Part VIII flags as untouched (~816 opinions). **That corpus is the empirical foundation of this bill and it does not exist yet.** Building it is the largest unbudgeted item here — and it is separately the most valuable extension of the underlying research project.
4. **Use the Court's own words.** The findings section should quote *Gorka*: the sanction was meant "to reduce litigation costs and conserve judicial resources," but "[t]he effect, however, **has been in sharp contrast to the intended outcome** because the statute and rule have seemingly increased litigation." And *Allen v. Nunez* (Polston, J., dissenting, with Canady, C.J., and Lawson, J.): the jurisprudence "**in this area of the law seems inconsistently applied and unpredictable**." ⭐ **A legislative finding that quotes three sitting-or-recent justices describing the regime as unpredictable is close to unanswerable in a rational-basis challenge**, and it reframes the bill as a response to judicial invitation rather than an industry favor.

**Opposition:** the Florida Justice Association, and — new since 2025 — an organized survivor constituency built around the "free kill" repeal, which is bipartisan, sympathetic, media-effective, and has now twice come within one step of a win. **That constituency did not exist when HB 837 passed in 2023. Do not model 2027 on 2023.**

## 6.5 The rulemaking flank

The Legislature is only one of the two institutions editing this field, and [`arc-matrix.md`](../arc-matrix.md) shows the Court is the more active one — eight rule amendments to the Legislature's three substantive statutory changes in forty years.

**Two moves follow.**

1. **File comments with the Civil Procedure Rules Committee.** If *Trace Elements* rehearing resolves and the invited (c)(3) amendment goes to the Committee, the coalition should be in that proceeding. A rule amendment that is side-neutral is worse for carriers than no amendment plus a statute — because it removes the problem the statute was going to solve while delivering none of the asymmetry.
2. **Anticipate the counter-move.** The documented 2003→2005→2010 loop shows the Court will amend the rule in response to a decision. It can equally amend the rule in response to a statute — as it did in 2022, conforming rule 1.442 to ch. 2022-271. ⚠️ **Any procedural component of this package can be undone by the Court within one rulemaking cycle without any constitutional confrontation at all.** That is the strongest practical argument for keeping every provision substantive. It is not just about surviving art. V; it is about being **unamendable by the other institution**.

# 7. The case against

Stated plainly, because a strategy paper that does not is not worth much.

1. **The empirical predicate may not support the bill.** Nobody has run the numbers. If the OIR closed-claim data shows med-mal defendants already recover § 768.79 fees at rates comparable to other tort defendants, the findings section collapses and *McCall*'s stale-findings problem arrives on day one.

2. **§ 768.79 may not be the binding constraint.** The carrier's real exposure in Florida medical malpractice is the uncapped noneconomic verdict, not the unrecovered defense fee. Scenarios A and B improve fee recovery at the margin; they do not touch the tail. **Only Scenario C addresses the actual problem** — and Scenario C is the hardest to pass and the least certain to survive. That is an uncomfortable but honest ranking: **value and feasibility run in opposite directions across these three.**

3. **The asymmetry in Scenario B is legible.** A zero-differential defense trigger against a 50% claimant trigger fits in a headline and a floor speech. There is no version of it that is not visibly one-sided, and the survivor constituency organized by the "free kill" fight will use it.

4. **⚠️ Article I, § 26 is genuinely unresolved.** No case in this project's corpus addresses whether a § 768.79 award against a med-mal claimant implicates it. The non-recourse mitigation is a workaround, not an answer, and it costs real deterrent value in the partial-recovery cases.

5. **The Court may fix apportionment first, for free.** *Trace Elements* invited a rule amendment. If the Committee delivers it, the most saleable element of Scenario A disappears — and with it the "we are just cleaning up a broken doctrine" framing that makes the rest palatable.

6. **A statute that passes can be narrowed by construction.** The regime's history is a Court repeatedly reading this statute more strictly than its text requires, and then — per [`memo.md`](../memo.md) Part VI.C — *someone else* correcting it. **The Legislature reached into this statute once, in 2022, and got a carve-out for one industry in one category of action.** That is the realistic ceiling on a first attempt. Plan for the second bill before the first one passes.

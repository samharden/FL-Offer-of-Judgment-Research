The timeline

Statute on the left, Court on the right — decisions and rulemaking distinguished because they are the Court acting in two different capacities, which is the whole subject.

The rewrite — and the birth of (c)(3)

Rule 1.442 becomes Proposals for Settlement. The committee note states the reason for the apportionment sentence in one line: “in order to conform with Fabre v. Marin.” The only amendment in the chain that answers to both tracks at once.

Allstate Indemnity Co. v. Hingson — the foundation stone

The singular word “party” in § 768.79(2)(b) is read to require apportionment, in two hedged sentences — “this, we believe” — over a dissent citing the Legislature’s own directive that the singular includes the plural. On a pre-1997 offer, so it had to be derived from the statute alone. Never revisited.

The Court refuses a fix

The Rules Committee proposes excusing apportionment where a party is only vicariously liable. The Court declines — expressly “in light of our decision in Willis Shaw.”

2005Decision

Lamb v. Matetzschk

Apportionment required even where liability is purely vicarious — exactly the problem the Committee had tried to prevent. Unanimous in result, but three justices write to say the rule is broken. Pariente, C.J.: the reading reaches “beyond Fabre” so that a joint offer to a husband and wife is no longer authorized.

Coates v. R.J. Reynolds

(c)(3) reopened — and left alone

A restyling amendment reaches the apportionment sentence and changes shall to must. Committee notes: no change — so the 1996 Fabre note still governs. Unanimous, and adopted while Trace Elements sat fully briefed on the Court’s own docket, four weeks before argument.

Loop — still open

The defect nobody has fixed

Identified by commenters in 1992 and called “beyond this Court’s control because substantive.” The Court asked the Legislature to act. It has not. The question has been answered by construction twice and amended never.

1992 defect flagged → 2006 Nichols construes it → 2013 Diamond Aircraft construes it again → 2026 still unamended

What the chronology shows

  • The rule is amended to fix a decision more often than to implement one. Of the four decision-driven rulemaking events, three were the Court correcting its own work — 1992, 2010, and 2022.
  • The two institutions move at very different rates. Four legislative touches in forty years against at least eight rule amendments. The Court is the active editor of this field; the Legislature intervenes rarely, late, and narrowly.
  • Only one amendment answers to both tracks at once — the 1996 rewrite. It is also the one that produced (c)(3).
  • Housekeeping outnumbers doctrine. Three of eight rule amendments respond to a decision and one to a statute; the rest are cross-references and restyling. The apportionment sentence has survived thirty years and four amendment cycles without its substance being revisited once.

Proposals for Settlement in Florida — A Practice Guide¶

Fla. Stat. § 768.79 · Fla. R. Civ. P. 1.442¶

Prepared: August 11, 2026 · Companion to memo.md, which explains why the regime looks like this. This guide explains how to use it.

Part 1 — What the device actually does¶

A proposal for settlement is a fee-shifting sanction for unreasonably rejecting a settlement offer. You serve a written proposal; if the other side rejects it and then does sufficiently worse at trial, they pay your fees and costs from the date the proposal was served.

Four features drive everything else:

1. Entitlement is arithmetic, not equitable. Once the numbers line up, the trial court has no discretion to deny fees on the ground that the rejection was reasonable. TGI Friday's, Inc. v. Dvorak, 663 So. 2d 606 (Fla. 1995), adopting Schmidt v. Fortner, 629 So. 2d 1036 (Fla. 4th DCA 1993). The court's discretion goes to the amount, not the entitlement.

2. There is exactly one escape hatch, and it is narrow. § 768.79(8)(a) (2024; (7)(a) in pre-2022 sources) lets the court disallow an award on a finding that the proposal was not made in good faith. The reasonableness factors at (8)(b) go to the amount. See Part 7.D.

3. You do not have to win. § 768.79 is not a prevailing-party statute. Coates v. R.J. Reynolds Tobacco Co., 365 So. 3d 353 (Fla. 2023). A defendant can recover fees even where the plaintiff takes a substantial judgment, so long as the judgment falls 25% below the defendant's proposal.

4. The requirements live in two instruments, and both bind you. The substantive right is in § 768.79; the procedure is in rule 1.442. Compliance with one is not compliance with the other — the classic casualty is a proposal that tracks the rule perfectly and never cites the statute. Campbell v. Goldman, 959 So. 2d 223 (Fla. 2007).

What the fee award is worth¶

Included Authority
Reasonable attorney's fees and costs incurred from the date of service of the proposal § 768.79(1)
Appellate fees Frosti v. Creel, 979 So. 2d 912 (Fla. 2008); Coates
Fees even though your client lost the case outright Coates
Excluded Authority
Any contingency risk multiplier Sarkis v. Allstate Ins. Co., 863 So. 2d 210 (Fla. 2003)

⚠️ Do not aggregate. If you served several separate proposals, you may not sum them and compare the total against the judgment. Anderson v. Hilton Hotels Corp., 202 So. 3d 846 (Fla. 2016). Each proposal stands or falls on its own.

Part 2 — The decision to serve¶

A. Plaintiff-side¶

Threshold: the judgment obtained must be at least 25% greater than your demand.

Price the demand off the judgment, not the verdict. "Judgment obtained" is a term of art (Part 8) — it is the net judgment plus your fees, taxable costs, and prejudgment interest as of the date of the offer. On an older, heavily-worked file, those accrued items can carry a demand over the threshold that the raw verdict would not.

Serve early, but not too early. Fees run from service, so an early proposal maximizes exposure. But the earlier you serve, the less you know about value — and a demand that turns out to be 25% below four-fifths of the judgment is simply money left on the table.

⚠️ Setoffs can be decisive. Because the comparison is arithmetic, a setoff of a few hundred dollars can eliminate entitlement outright. In Rollins v. Pizzarelli, 761 So. 2d 294 (Fla. 2000), a PIP setoff moved a $25,048 judgment to $24,523.22 and destroyed the fee claim on a $20,000 demand. Model your comparator net of every setoff you can foresee — PIP deductibles, collateral sources, Fabre allocations, prior settlements.

B. Defendant-side¶

Threshold: the judgment is one of no liability, or the judgment obtained by the plaintiff is at least 25% less than your proposal.

A defense verdict satisfies the threshold automatically. That has been true only since October 1, 1990 — before ch. 90-119 § 48, the statute measured against "the judgment obtained by the plaintiff," so defendants could not recover on a defense verdict. Irrelevant to a modern file; relevant if you are dealing with an old accrual date (Part 10.D).

The nominal proposal is legal but untested. Nothing squarely forbids a $1 proposal, and one went unchallenged in Frosti. But the Court flagged doubt about "a one dollar offer of settlement" as early as Leapai v. Milton, 595 So. 2d 12 (Fla. 1992) (n.2), and no decision in the Supreme Court corpus squarely resolves a good-faith challenge. Treat a nominal proposal as carrying real, undecided risk — see Part 12.A.

Differentiated proposals among codefendants are legitimate. Anderson records the ordinary strategic reasons approvingly: to force one defendant to settle, to "eliminate the defendant for whom the jury may have sympathy," or to remove the defendant with the best lawyer. And motive is irrelevant to facial validity — it goes to good faith only. Anderson.

C. The cost of getting it wrong¶

Two asymmetries worth putting in front of a client:

  • An invalid proposal costs you the fee claim you were building — but it does not otherwise hurt you.
  • Rejecting a valid proposal that you then fail to beat by the margin can transfer the entire post-service cost of the litigation. On a case tried two years after service, that is usually the largest single number in the file.

The Court itself has said the device increases litigation rather than reducing it. Attorneys' Title Ins. Fund, Inc. v. Gorka, 36 So. 3d 646, 650 (Fla. 2010): the intended effect "has been in sharp contrast to the intended outcome." Budget for a fight over validity.

Part 3 — The calendar¶

A. The service window — rule 1.442(b)¶

Event Earliest Latest
Proposal to a defendant 90 days after service of process on that defendant 45 days before the trial date or the first day of the trial docket, whichever is earlier
Proposal to a plaintiff 90 days after the action was commenced same

⚠️ Mediation does not move these dates. Rule 1.442(j). The Legislature tried to alter the timing through § 44.102(6) and the Court struck the attempt as purely procedural. Knealing v. Puleo, 675 So. 2d 593 (Fla. 1996).

⚠️ A continuance changes the outside date. The 45-day limit runs off the trial setting, so a reset trial date reopens the window. Diary it against the current order setting trial, not the original one.

B. The acceptance clock — rule 1.442(f)(1)¶

  • 30 days. A proposal is deemed rejected unless a written acceptance is delivered within 30 days of service.
  • 🔴 The extra-days-for-service provision does not apply. Rule 1.442(f)(1) expressly excludes Fla. R. Jud. Admin. 2.514(b). Thirty days means thirty days — no five-day add-on, whatever the method of service.
  • 🔴 A motion to enlarge does not toll the clock. Koppel v. Ochoa, 243 So. 3d 886 (Fla. 2018), disapproving Goldy v. Corbett Cranes after nineteen years of contrary practice. The offeree in Koppel filed one day before expiry, got a hearing two months later, and purported to accept on day 90. It did not work.
  • No oral communication constitutes acceptance, rejection, or counteroffer. Rule 1.442(f)(1).

C. The fee-motion deadline — rule 1.442(g) and rule 1.525¶

30 days, and the two instruments measure from different events:

Instrument Runs from
Rule 1.442(g) — jury action Return of the verdict
Rule 1.442(g) — nonjury action Entry of judgment
§ 768.79 Entry of judgment

The rule prevails where it differs from the statute, because the timing is procedural. Gulliver Academy, Inc. v. Bodek, 694 So. 2d 675 (Fla. 1997). In a jury trial, calendar from the verdict.

See Part 9 for what the 30 days does and does not tolerate.

Part 4 — 🔴 Joint proposals¶

This is where practitioners lose most often. Read this part even if you think your proposal is not joint.

A. The rule¶

Rule 1.442(c)(3): a proposal may be made by or to any party or parties and any combination of them, but "[a] joint proposal must state the amount and terms attributable to each party."

Rule 1.442(c)(4) is the one exception: where a party is alleged to be solely vicariously, constructively, derivatively, or technically liable, a joint proposal made by or served on that party need not apportion as to that party. Adopted in 2010 to undo Lamb v. Matetzschk, 906 So. 2d 1037 (Fla. 2005).

B. When is a proposal "joint"? — the operative test¶

⭐ A proposal becomes joint when it speaks for or binds another party — not merely because another party has claims pending. Anderson (2016) and Allen v. Nunez, 258 So. 3d 1207 (Fla. 2018).

The line is drawn precisely by comparing two cases:

Audiffred v. Arnold, 161 So. 3d 1274 (Fla. 2015) Anderson (2016)
Recital resolves "[a]ny and all claims Plaintiffs have brought" "PLAINTIFF," defined as Troy Anderson alone
Required of the non-offeror spouse both spouses had to dismiss never mentioned
Result joint — apportionment required, proposal invalid not joint — "[a]pportionment simply was not an issue"

Practical rule: police your defined terms and your dismissal language. A plural noun, an undefined "Plaintiffs," or a release/dismissal clause that reaches a non-offeror converts a single-party proposal into a joint one that must apportion.

Two further boundary points:

  • A single offeror can make a "joint" proposal if it resolves claims by or against parties who are neither offerors nor offerees. Audiffred.
  • A proposal captioned from one entity but written in the plural is joint. Pratt v. Weiss, 161 So. 3d 1268 (Fla. 2015) — a limited partnership and its general partner are distinct entities, and the text controlled over the caption.

C. Apportionment is required even when it makes no sense¶

  • "Even where no logical apportionment can be made, it is nonetheless required where more than one offeror or offeree is involved." Pratt, 161 So. 3d at 1272–73. Difficulty, coextensive liability, and the ability to apportion after acceptance are "not recognized legal elements to disregard or circumvent the rule."
  • "It may take some creative drafting." Lamb.
  • 🔴 Required even for a unified, single claim held by spouses. Trace Elements (Fla. July 2, 2026), 4–3 — subject to the pending rehearing motion. The proposal there was a joint, unapportioned $10,000 from a husband and wife; the verdict was $41,273.70; the fee claim failed on (c)(3).

Spouses are the recurring casualty. Allstate Indemnity Co. v. Hingson, 808 So. 2d 197 (Fla. 2002); D'Angelo v. Fitzmaurice, 863 So. 2d 311 (Fla. 2003); Audiffred; Trace Elements — four sets of spouses, plus the Herzogs in the case Hingson disapproved. There is no spousal-unity exception. Pariente, C.J., objected in 2005 that the rule as construed means "a joint offer to a husband and wife is no longer authorized" (Lamb, specially concurring); the objection has never been adopted.

D. Conditioning acceptance — what is and is not allowed¶

🔴 One offeror → multiple offerees, conditioned on all of them accepting: INVALID. Gorka (2010). "An offer that cannot be unilaterally accepted to create a binding settlement is an illusory offer" — it divests each offeree of independent control.

✅ Multiple offerors → one offeree, conditioned on that offeree accepting as to all offerors: approved by the district courts. The "all-or-nothing allocated offer." Hoang Dinh Duong v. Ziadie, 153 So. 3d 354, 358–60 (Fla. 4th DCA 2014), collecting Wolfe v. Culpepper Constructors (2d DCA 2012), Rossmore v. Smith (5th DCA 2011), Andrews v. Frey (5th DCA 2011), and Donovan Marine v. Delmonico (4th DCA 2010).

The distinction is structural, and it is why Gorka does not reach the second structure:

Gorka All-or-nothing allocated offer
Direction one offeror → multiple offerees multiple offerors → one offeree
Condition all offerees must accept the single offeree must accept as to all offerors
Independent control destroyed intact — there is only one decision-maker
Result invalid valid

⚠️ Two cautions before you rely on the collection. Andrews v. Frey was later disapproved by the Supreme Court in Audiffred — on a different question (sole-offeror vs. joint proposal), not on the all-or-nothing structure, but say so if you cite it. And the Supreme Court has not itself passed on the structure.

⚠️ The statutory carve-out is narrow. § 768.79(6) (2022) permits a conditional joint offer only where a property insurer makes it in a breach of contract action. It is not a general relaxation of Gorka, and it is not an apportionment provision. Do not describe it as one.

E. ⚠️ The price of an all-or-nothing allocated offer: the 25% test becomes conjunctive¶

This is the most under-appreciated consequence in the field, and it is stated plainly in Hoang Dinh Duong:

if the verdict for any claimant was not 25% higher than that claimant's allocated share, none of the claimants recovers fees. "[I]t was not enough that the total amount of the verdict exceed the total amount of the offer by twenty-five percent."

Allocate deliberately. Loading one claimant heavily to make the aggregate look reasonable can hand the offeree a defense: if that claimant underperforms at trial, the whole fee claim fails, however well the others did.

C. The particularity standard — Nichols¶

The operative test, and the one the trial court will apply:

A proposal must be "sufficiently clear and definite to allow the offeree to make an informed decision without needing clarification." The rule "does not demand the impossible." "If ambiguity within the proposal could reasonably affect the offeree's decision, the proposal will not satisfy the particularity requirement."

⭐ And the qualifier that saves most releases: "The rule aims to prevent ambiguity, not breadth." A sweeping general release is fine if it is unambiguous. Summarize it if you like — a summary suffices "as long as it eliminates any reasonable ambiguity about its scope."

Two rules of construction that work in the drafter's favor:

  • The proposal is read as a whole. An arguably loose recital in one paragraph is cured by the rest of the document. Allen v. Nunez.
  • Courts are discouraged from "nitpicking." Anderson and Allen both reversed district courts for manufacturing ambiguity, in the same words: the courts below "unnecessarily injected ambiguity into these proceedings and created more judicial labor, not less."
  • A transmittal letter is not a second, conflicting proposal. Reading it as one is "grammatically possible" but "substantively unreasonable." Hoang Dinh Duong. Still — do not put settlement terms in the cover letter.

D. Attach and align¶

  • If a release is referenced, attach it. Do not incorporate a document the offeree has not seen.
  • Check that the release's scope, the (c)(2)(B) all-damages recital, and any (c)(2)(C) conditions do not contradict each other.
  • Check that no term reaches a party who is not named as an offeror or offeree — that is what converts the proposal to a joint one (Part 4.B).

Part 6 — Serving the proposal¶

Point Authority
Proposals are served, not filed, unless filing is necessary to enforce the rule rule 1.442(d)
Premature filing is not fatal. The remedy is a motion to strike, with leave to refile Frosti
⭐ Proposals are NOT subject to the e-mail service requirements of Fla. R. Jud. Admin. 2.516 — a proposal is neither a pleading nor a document filed in a court proceeding Wheaton v. Wheaton, 261 So. 3d 1236 (Fla. 2019)
The 30-day acceptance period gets no extra days for the method of service rule 1.442(f)(1) (excluding rule 2.514(b))

⚠️ Practical advice notwithstanding Wheaton: serve by e-mail in conformity with rule 2.516 anyway, and keep the certificate. Wheaton's primary holding is that 2.516 does not apply; its alternative holding — that noncompliance would be excused because the proposal satisfied § 768.79's substantive requirements — applies a substantive-compliance test broader than anything in Kuhajda, and the Supreme Court has not revisited it since 2019. Trace Elements does not mention it. Do not build a strategy on the alternative holding.

Prove service. Include the (c)(2)(G) certificate, keep the transmission record, and calendar day 30 the moment it goes out.

Part 7 — Receiving a proposal¶

A. Calendar first, analyze second¶

Day 1 is service. Day 30 is the deadline. No add-on days. No tolling by motion. Koppel. Put the date in the file, on the calendar, and in the client letter the same day.

B. Deciding¶

Model the comparator honestly, using the White formula (Part 8) — not the verdict you hope for. On a plaintiff-side file, ask: is there a realistic verdict at which the judgment obtained, computed as of today, falls below 75% of this proposal? On a defense file: above 125% of the demand?

⚠️ Accepting a proposal silent as to fees ends the fee claim too. An accepted offer of judgment that says nothing about attorney's fees terminates the litigation and forecloses a later motion for statutory fees; statutory fee rights can be waived. Unicare Health Facilities, Inc. v. Mort, 553 So. 2d 159 (Fla. 1989) (the "organic right of parties to contract a settlement, which by definition concludes all claims unless the contract of settlement specifies otherwise"). If you have a separate fee entitlement, do not accept a silent proposal without resolving it.

C. Mechanics of acceptance, rejection, and withdrawal¶

Action Rule
Acceptance Written notice delivered within 30 days — rule 1.442(f)(1). A filed written acceptance forms a binding settlement contract; acceptance is not performance. Suarez Trucking FL Corp. v. Souders, No. SC21-369 (Fla. Oct. 20, 2022): "Once a proper acceptance … is filed as specified in the statute, that's it: a settlement contract has been entered."
The acceptance need not recite the offer's terms The mirror-image rule is "a rule of consistency. It is not … a rule of regurgitation." Suarez Trucking. But an acceptance must be unqualified — do not add terms.
Rejection Automatic. A proposal is deemed rejected if not accepted in 30 days. You need do nothing.
Counteroffer Does not terminate the proposal. Scope v. Fannelli, 639 So. 2d 141 (Fla. 5th DCA 1994). The original proposal remains open until written withdrawal or expiry — which cuts both ways: it means negotiating does not forfeit your right to accept, and it means an offeror who wants the proposal dead must withdraw it in writing.
Withdrawal In writing, before a written acceptance is delivered; once withdrawn the proposal is void. Rule 1.442(e).
Oral anything Irrelevant. Rule 1.442(f)(1).

⚠️ The framework "does not envision a process of negotiation." Proposals are "by their very nature take-it-or-leave-it propositions." Suarez Trucking. Side conversations about terms are legally inert; only the writing counts.

D. Attacking a proposal served on you¶

The grounds that have actually worked, in rough order of success rate:

  1. Failure to apportion a joint proposal — rule 1.442(c)(3). Part 4. The largest category by a wide margin. Look first at defined terms, plural nouns, and any clause that binds a non-offeror.
  2. Conditioned on mutual acceptance by multiple offerees — Gorka. Check whether the § 768.79(6) property-insurer carve-out applies before arguing this.
  3. No citation to § 768.79 — Campbell.
  4. Ambiguity that could reasonably affect the decision — Nichols. ⚠️ But read Anderson and Allen first: the Court has twice reversed courts for manufacturing ambiguity, and the proposal is read as a whole. A nitpicking argument is now more likely to draw a rebuke than a win.
  5. (c)(2)(F) omission where fees were pleaded — Diamond Aircraft. Check the complaint; if fees were not pleaded, Kuhajda forecloses this.
  6. The action is not a "civil action for damages" — Part 10.
  7. Good faith — § 768.79(8)(a). See below.
  8. The comparison fails — run the White formula yourself (Part 8) rather than accepting the movant's arithmetic. Setoffs are where these are won.

The good-faith challenge¶

This is the only discretionary escape, and it is the least-developed part of the regime — which cuts both ways. There is little authority to cite, and little authority against you.

  • Motive is not a validity question. Even an admission that a proposal was aimed at settling someone else's claim goes to good faith, not facial validity. Anderson.
  • Differentiated and strategic offers are legitimate. Anderson, quoting Hess v. Walton.
  • Nominal proposals are the open question. Leapai n.2 (1992) raised doubt about "a one dollar offer of settlement" and said the issue "was neither presented nor addressed." Frosti (2008) let a $1 punitive proposal pass unchallenged. No Supreme Court decision in the corpus squarely decides a good-faith challenge. If you are attacking a nominal proposal, you are litigating on open ground — build the record in the trial court, because that is where the finding will be made.

Part 8 — Running the 25% comparison¶

A. The thresholds¶

Offeror Entitled if…
Defendant judgment is one of no liability, or the judgment obtained by the plaintiff is at least 25% less than the defendant's proposal
Plaintiff the judgment obtained is at least 25% greater than the plaintiff's demand

§ 768.79(1); the post-judgment determination procedure is at § 768.79(7) (2024) — (6) in any pre-2022 source.

B. "Judgment obtained" is a term of art — the White formula¶

Not the verdict. White v. Steak and Ale of Florida, Inc., 816 So. 2d 546 (Fla. 2002):

judgment obtained  =  net judgment for damages
                   +  attorney's fees        ┐
                   +  taxable costs          ├ as they stood ON THE DATE OF THE OFFER
                   +  prejudgment interest   ┘

Three refinements:

Refinement Authority
Post-offer prejudgment interest is EXCLUDED CCM Condominium Ass'n v. Petri Positive Pest Control, No. SC19-861 (Fla. Sept. 9, 2021)
The whole net judgment counts — you may not isolate a favorable slice (e.g. the compensatory portion) to defeat the threshold Frosti
Separate proposals may not be aggregated and compared against the judgment Anderson

⚠️ The formula has never been examined on the merits. In CCM, the Fourth District below said the plain meaning of § 768.79 does not support the formula but considered itself bound; the Supreme Court applied State v. Poole's "clearly erroneous" standard and declined to look. Canady, C.J., dissenting, argued the formula's supporting authorities were § 627.428 cases with "no relevant textual similarity." It is settled law and you should apply it — but it is settled on stare decisis, not on text.

C. Worked examples¶

Simple, single claimant. Defendant serves a $100,000 proposal. Verdict: $70,000. As of the date of the offer, plaintiff's taxable costs and prejudgment interest totaled $8,000.

judgment obtained = 70,000 + 8,000 = 78,000
75% of 100,000                     = 75,000
78,000 > 75,000  ──►  defendant is NOT entitled.

Note how the $8,000 of pre-offer accruals — not the verdict — decided it. Compute the accruals before you set the number.

Unified claim, unapportioned joint proposal. Trace Elements: spouses served a joint, unapportioned $10,000 proposal; the verdict was $41,273.70 — comfortably past 125%. The fee claim still failed, on (c)(3), because the proposal was not apportioned. Arithmetic success does not survive a drafting defect.

All-or-nothing allocated proposal — the conjunctive test. Hoang Dinh Duong: $1,000,000 total, apportioned $900,000 / $50,000 / $50,000, served on one defendant. Each claimant's judgment had to beat that claimant's allocated share by 25%, or no one recovered fees. They did, and $557,452 in fees was affirmed. Run the test per claimant, not on the total.

Part 9 — Claiming the fees¶

A. The deadline¶

Serve the motion in accordance with rule 1.525 — rule 1.442(g). 30 days. See Part 3.C for the measuring event (verdict in a jury case).

Point Authority
🔴 A reservation of jurisdiction in the final judgment does NOT extend rule 1.525's 30 days Saia Motor Freight Line, Inc. v. Reid, 930 So. 2d 598 (Fla. 2006)
But 1.525 is a ceiling, not a window — a motion served before judgment is timely Frosti, following Barco v. School Bd. of Pinellas County
Prospective-only application is not available This line has never been applied prospectively — sought and refused in White, Saia, and Koppel

Practical consequence: serve early. There is no penalty for serving the motion before judgment and a fatal one for serving it late. Saia and Gulliver Academy were decided nine years apart by the same author on nearly the same facts, and reconcile only because rule 1.525 was adopted in between. Do not rely on a reservation of jurisdiction to save you.

B. Pleading¶

⭐ A § 768.79 fee claim is not subject to Stockman v. Downs's pleading requirement, because entitlement arises from an event occurring during the suit rather than from the nature of the claim. Discussed in Advanced Chiropractic & Rehabilitation Center, Corp. v. United Automobile Ins. Co., 140 So. 3d 529 (Fla. 2014), on district authority collected there. ⚠️ The Supreme Court has not squarely held this in a § 768.79 case in the read corpus — plead the entitlement anyway if you have any basis to; there is no cost to doing so.

C. Amount¶

  • No contingency risk multiplier. Sarkis.
  • The § 768.79(8)(b) factors (2024; (7)(b) pre-2022) go to the amount, not entitlement. TGI Friday's/Schmidt.
  • Appellate fees are recoverable. Frosti; Coates. Follow rule 9.400 and move in the appellate court.

Part 10 — When § 768.79 does not apply at all¶

Check these before you spend an hour drafting.

A. Actions seeking damages and equitable relief¶

§ 768.79 does not apply to an action seeking both damages and equitable relief, at least where the proposal is a general one resolving all claims. Diamond Aircraft. There is no exception for an equitable claim that lacks serious merit, and pleading in the alternative does not help — "an equitable claim in the alternative to a monetary claim is still part of the same civil action."

⭐ The open workaround. Diamond Aircraft expressly reserved whether the result changes where the monetary and nonmonetary claims are pled separately and the proposal is directed only to the monetary claim. That question is still undecided. It is a real drafting opportunity and a real risk — see Part 12.B.

B. Contractual choice of another state's law¶

§ 768.79 is substantive for conflict-of-law purposes. Where the parties contracted to be bound by another state's substantive law, § 768.79 does not apply. Southeast Floating Docks, Inc. v. Auto-Owners Ins. Co., 82 So. 3d 73 (Fla. 2012). Check the contract before you serve.

C. Voluntary dismissal without prejudice¶

Fees follow only where a plaintiff's voluntary dismissal is with prejudice, or is a second voluntary dismissal. MX Investments, Inc. v. Crawford, 700 So. 2d 640 (Fla. 1997). A rule 1.420(d) cost judgment is not a "judgment" for this purpose.

D. Old accrual dates¶

§ 768.79 does not reach causes of action accruing before July 1, 1986 (§ 768.71(2)), and the applicable version is the one in effect when the cause of action accrued — not when the offer was served. Metropolitan Dade County v. Jones Boatyard, Inc., 611 So. 2d 512 (Fla. 1993).

⚠️ § 45.061 was never repealed. It was repealed prospectively in 1990 as to causes accruing after October 1, 1990, and survives on the books as § 45.061, Fla. Stat., for pre-October-1990 accruals. Vestigial, but if you are litigating a very old claim, check it — its § 45.061(2)(b) definition of "the amount of the judgment" differs from the White formula, and its temporal trigger is the making of the offer, not accrual.

E. Things that are not exclusions¶

  • PIP and other contractual-damages suits are within the statute. "[N]othing in the offer of judgment statute exempts claims for contractual damages." Nichols.
  • You need not prevail. Coates.
  • A pending counterclaim does not defeat an unqualified proposal — it is resolved by it. MGR Equipment.

Part 11 — Traps, ranked¶

Ordered by how often they appear as the dispositive defect in the Supreme Court corpus.

# Trap Fix
1 🔴 A joint proposal that does not apportion — including spouses, unified claims, and claims where apportionment is illogical Part 4. Apportion, or restructure so the proposal binds only its named offeror/offeree
2 🔴 Citing rule 1.442 without citing § 768.79 Cite both, in the first sentence. Campbell
3 🔴 Assuming a motion to enlarge tolls the 30-day acceptance period It does not. Koppel

8 Expecting fees after a voluntary dismissal without prejudice MX Investments

The numbering trap¶

🔴 Chapter 2022-271 renumbered § 768.79. There are two different subsection (6)s.

Provision Pre-2022 2024
Entitlement (1) (1)
Offer requirements (2) (2)
Property-insurer conditional joint offers did not exist (6)
Judgment comparison / the 25% math (6) (7)
Good faith (7)(a) (8)(a)
Reasonableness factors (amount) (7)(b) (8)(b)

Every pin cite must be tied to a statutory year, and the governing version keys to the accrual date (Jones Boatyard), not the date of the offer or today's book. A brief citing "§ 768.79(6)" without a year is ambiguous on its face — and if it is quoting a pre-2022 case, it almost certainly means what is now (7).

The terminology trap (for research)¶

Pre-1996 opinions say "offer of judgment" or "demand for judgment." Post-1996 they say "proposal for settlement." Searching on the modern phrase silently truncates the case law at 1997. Search on "768.79" and "1.442."

Part 12 — Unsettled ground¶

Where the law is genuinely open. Do not build a client strategy on any of these without disclosing the risk.

A. Good faith is the least-developed area in the regime¶

Five decisions touch it and four are asides. No decision in the read Supreme Court corpus squarely resolves a good-faith challenge. Since good faith is the only discretionary escape from an otherwise mechanical entitlement, this is simultaneously the biggest gap and the biggest opportunity. The nominal-proposal question — flagged by the Court in 1992 and never answered — is the sharpest edge of it.

B. The Diamond Aircraft reservation¶

Whether § 768.79 applies where monetary and nonmonetary claims are pled separately and the proposal is directed only to the monetary claim. Expressly left open in 2013 and still open. A tempting workaround; an untested one.

C. Wheaton's alternative holding¶

Wheaton excused noncompliance with a different rule set (2.516) on a substantive-compliance rationale broader than Kuhajda's. Trace Elements — decided seven years later and squarely about when strict compliance applies — does not mention it. Its continued vitality is unresolved.

D. The White formula¶

Applied as settled law, but sustained in CCM on stare decisis under State v. Poole rather than on the text, over a dissent calling its supporting authorities "of very dubious provenance," and after the district court below said the plain language does not support it. Apply it. Do not assume it is permanent.

E. Trace Elements itself¶

🔴 Not final; rehearing and clarification pending as of August 8, 2026. Two further points:

  • The majority expressly invited a rule amendment rather than a judicial exception, citing the 2010 adoption of (c)(4) after Lamb as its model. The Civil Procedure Rules Committee has form here: a proposed (c)(3) carve-out was rejected in 2003, the problem it would have prevented arose in 2005, and the Court adopted essentially the original proposal in 2010. Watch for a (c)(3) amendment proposal.
  • The Court restyled (c)(3) unanimously on November 13, 2025 — changing only shall to must, Committee Notes unchanged — while Trace Elements sat fully briefed on its own docket, four weeks before oral argument. Whatever else follows, the current text is the text the Court re-adopted knowing this case was coming.

F. The Court's own assessment¶

Worth quoting to a client who asks why this is so hard:

"this Court's jurisprudence in this area of the law seems inconsistently applied and unpredictable" — Polston, J., dissenting in Allen v. Nunez (2018), joined by Canady, C.J., and Lawson, J.

The majority's response, in footnote 2, does not dispute the diagnosis — only what follows from it.

Quick-reference — one-page drafting checklist¶

Before drafting

  • Is this a "civil action for damages"? Any equitable claim in the case? (Diamond Aircraft)
  • Does any contract choose another state's substantive law? (Southeast Floating Docks)
  • When did the cause of action accrue? Which statutory version governs? (Jones Boatyard)
  • Are we inside the rule 1.442(b) window — 90 days after / 45 days before, off the current trial setting?
  • Have I modeled the comparator under the White formula, net of foreseeable setoffs?

In the document

  • Cites § 768.79 by name — not just rule 1.442
  • Names the offeror and the offeree; defined terms are singular unless a joint proposal is intended
  • Nothing in the document speaks for, binds, or requires anything of a non-party to the proposal
  • States that it resolves all damages that would otherwise be awarded in final judgment
  • Every condition stated with particularity; any release attached
  • Total amount stated; every nonmonetary term stated with particularity
  • Punitive damages amount stated separately, if claimed
  • Attorney's-fees statement included — whether fees are included and whether they are part of the legal claim
  • Certificate of service
  • Nothing material in the cover letter

If joint

  • Amount and terms attributable to each party stated
  • (c)(4) vicarious-liability exception applied only where liability is solely vicarious/constructive/derivative/technical
  • If conditioned: multiple offerors → one offeree only. Never multiple offerees conditioned on mutual acceptance (Gorka), absent § 768.79(6)
  • Allocation stress-tested against the conjunctive 25% test — would a weak result for one claimant sink all of them?

On service

  • Serve, do not file (rule 1.442(d))
  • Calendar day 30 — no add-on days, no tolling by motion
  • Calendar the rule 1.525 fee-motion deadline from the verdict (jury) or entry of judgment (nonjury)

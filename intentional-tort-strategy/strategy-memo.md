# 1. The asymmetry problem

**Start with the uncomfortable fact: § 768.79 is symmetric on its face.** A claimant who serves a demand and beats it by 25% recovers fees exactly as a defendant does. In an intentional-tort case against a solvent institution that refuses to engage, a well-timed demand is real leverage, and any proposal to "protect victims" by switching the statute off takes that leverage away too. Part 9.1 treats this as the strongest objection in the memo, because it is.

**But the burden is not symmetric in practice, and the reasons are structural rather than rhetorical.**

## 1.1 The valuation problem is at its worst here

Entitlement is arithmetic. [*TGI Friday's, Inc. v. Dvorak*, 663 So. 2d 606 (Fla. 1995)](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1715682/tgi-fridays-inc-v-dvorak/) settled that once the numbers line up, the court has no discretion to deny fees because the rejection was reasonable. So everything turns on whether a party can predict the verdict to within 25% at the time it answers a proposal.

The intentional-tort damages profile is the hardest case for that. Economic damages are frequently small — a sexual battery claimant may have no wage loss and modest medicals. The recovery is noneconomic and punitive, which is to say it is whatever a jury decides it is. And liability is often credibility-dependent, so the distribution is not merely wide but **bimodal**: a large verdict or nothing. A claimant answering a $50,000 proposal in a case that will produce either $2 million or zero is not making a valuation judgment. **The 25% differential was designed to absorb ordinary valuation error. It does not absorb a bimodal distribution, and the statute contains no mechanism that notices the difference.**

## 1.2 The punitive-damages proposal is a one-way instrument

Rule 1.442(c)(2)(E) requires a proposal to "state with particularity the amount proposed to settle a claim for punitive damages, if any." A defendant satisfies that by naming a nominal figure. [*Frosti v. Creel*, 979 So. 2d 912 (Fla. 2008)](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1785275/frosti-v-creel/) involved a **$1 proposal** to settle a punitive claim, and no good-faith challenge to it reached the Court.

So a defendant can serve a proposal that prices the punitive claim at nothing, and the claimant must then beat the aggregate by 25% or pay. Meanwhile **§ 768.72(1) bars the claimant from even pleading punitive damages** until the court finds "a reasonable showing by evidence in the record or proffered by the claimant which would provide a reasonable basis for recovery of such damages." A proposal served before that ruling asks the claimant to price a claim the claimant is not yet permitted to assert.

⭐ **This is the sharpest single point available to the claimant side, and it is not an argument about fairness. It is an argument that the statute's own arithmetic is being run on a number that Florida law forbids the claimant to have yet.** It supports Scenario A directly and it is the reason A-2 is worth its constitutional risk.

## 1.3 The proposals come from the party that is not the wrongdoer

Intentional acts are ordinarily excluded from liability coverage. The individual perpetrator is therefore usually uninsured, frequently judgment-proof, and often incarcerated — and makes no proposals, because there is no defense budget behind them.

The proposals come from the **institutional co-defendant** — the employer, school, church, hotel, apartment complex, staffing agency, transport company — sued in negligent hiring, retention, supervision, or security, and defended by a carrier. That defendant is insured, represented, and has both the money and the incentive to serve early proposals and run up a fee claim.

Two consequences:

- **The fee exposure runs against the victim and in favor of the entity that enabled the conduct**, which is not what "loser pays for unreasonable refusal" is usually understood to mean.
- The claim against the institution sounds in **negligence**, so a carve-out written around the *claim* pleaded reaches only the perpetrator — against whom § 768.79 exposure was never the practical problem. **Any carve-out that is not drafted around the underlying conduct will miss the defendant that matters.** This is the central drafting constraint in Part 5.

## 1.4 The good-faith escape does not work

Section 768.79(8)(a) — the only discretionary escape — is the emptiest arc in the corpus. [`../arc-matrix.md`](../arc-matrix.md) finding #2: five entries across thirty-nine merits decisions, and **four are asides** — *Leapai* n.2's 1992 doubt about "a one dollar offer," *Frosti*'s unchallenged $1 punitive proposal, *Anderson*'s holding that motive goes to good faith rather than validity, and *Coates*'s reliance on the (8)(b) factors. **No decision in the read corpus squarely resolves a good-faith challenge.**

A vacuum is not neutral. It means the question is resolved by trial-court discretion, case by case, with no appellate standard — and the party asking a trial judge to exercise discretion in its favour is the party that already lost the arithmetic.

⚠️ Note the symmetry, honestly: the sibling carrier memo identifies the same vacuum and proposes to fill it with a **presumption of good faith**. Both sides have located the same hole. **The empty arc is the most contested unoccupied ground in the statute, and whoever legislates into it first will hold it for a long time**, because arc 6 has produced no doctrine in forty years to displace.

# 2. The six levers

| # | Lever | Current state | Claimant-favorable direction |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Scope** — § 768.79(1), "any civil action for damages" | Applies to every damages action, intentional torts included | **Exclude, or partially exclude, the intentional-misconduct class** |
| **2** | **Directionality** | Symmetric | **One-way: claimant retains entitlement, defendant loses it** |
| **3** | **Good faith** — § 768.79(8)(a) | Empty | **Legislate a floor: nominal and unsupported proposals presumed not in good faith** |
| **4** | **The comparator** — § 768.79(7), the *White* formula | Judgment obtained = damages + fees + costs + prejudgment interest at the offer date | **Codify that punitive damages count; deny entitlement to proposals served before the § 768.72 ruling** |
| **5** | **Recourse** | A fee award is an ordinary collectible judgment | **Make it non-recourse against a natural-person claimant who obtains a judgment** |
| **6** | **The defendant's own exposure** | Fees run from the proposal | **Run the claimant's fees from a presuit written demand**, on the § 772.11 model |

⭐ **Levers 1 and 4 are where the value is.** Lever 3 is the cheapest. Lever 2 is the hardest to pass and the least necessary. Lever 5 is a concession chip. Lever 6 is the affirmative ask that makes the package look like something other than a shield.

# 3. The four gates

## Gate 1 — Article V, § 2(a): substance or procedure

**The test**, from [*Massey v. David*, 979 So. 2d 931 (Fla. 2008)](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1786026/massey-v-david/), quoting *Haven Federal Savings & Loan v. Kirian*: substantive law "creates, defines, and regulates rights"; procedure is "the course, form, manner, means, method, mode, order, process or steps by which a party enforces substantive rights."

**Applied here**, [*Knealing v. Puleo*, 675 So. 2d 593 (Fla. 1996)](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1725782/knealing-v-puleo/): §§ 45.061 and 768.79 survived because each contains a substantive fee-authorizing provision; **§ 44.102(6) did not**, because the Legislature tried to alter proposal *timing* through the mediation statute.

⭐ **The claimant side is structurally advantaged at this gate, and the reason is worth stating precisely.**

The carrier-side memo has to work hard here, because most of what a carrier wants is a change to how proposals operate — and operation is the Court's. **What the claimant side wants is mostly the removal or narrowing of an entitlement, and an entitlement is the most unambiguously substantive thing in the statute.** *Knealing* holds that § 768.79 survives *because* it substantively authorizes fees. The corollary is that the Legislature owns that authorization and can withdraw it.

⭐⭐ **And the Court has said so, on this exact clause.** In the *Timmons* **Supplemental Order of October 22, 1992**, commenters complained that § 768.79 reaches only "civil actions for damages." The Court answered that this is "**beyond this Court's control because [it is] substantive**," and referred the matter to the Rules Committee for a new rule instead. [`../memo.md`](../memo.md) Part VII.B records the sequel: the scope defect has been resolved **by construction** twice — *Nichols* (2006) and *Diamond Aircraft* (2013) — and **amended never, in thirty-four years.**

**So an amendment to the scope clause of § 768.79(1) is (i) squarely substantive, (ii) responsive to an express judicial statement that this is the Legislature's job, and (iii) unamendable by rule** — because a fee entitlement in this field must originate in a substantive statute, the Court cannot restore by rulemaking an entitlement the statute no longer grants. That is a durability property nothing in the carrier memo has.

🔴 **The one drafting instruction that matters:** never write a provision that regulates *the content, form, or timing of a proposal*. Write provisions that regulate *entitlement*. "A defendant may not serve a proposal while a motion under s. 768.72 is pending" is a timing rule and is *Knealing* on all fours. "**Entitlement under this section does not arise from a proposal served while a motion under s. 768.72 is pending and undetermined**" reaches the same result and is an entitlement rule. The distinction is not cosmetic; it decides the case.

## Gate 2 — Access to courts, and why it is not a gate here

Article I, § 21 and ⚠️ *Kluger v. White*, 281 So. 2d 1 (Fla. 1973), constrain the Legislature's power to *abolish* a right of redress without a reasonable alternative or an overpowering public necessity. That doctrine is the central obstacle in the carrier memo's Scenario C, which would cap damages.

**Every scenario here runs the other way.** Removing a fee-shifting exposure enlarges access; it does not restrict it. A defendant has no constitutional interest in recovering attorney's fees — the American rule is that fees are not recoverable absent statute or contract, and what a statute gives it may take.

**Gate 2 is open. Note that this is unusual, and it is the main reason the constitutional odds in Part 7 run high across the board.**

## Gate 3 — Equal protection

**Rational basis.** Economic and remedial legislation.

Two classifications are in play, and they are not equally strong:

**(a) Classification by the defendant's conduct — strong.** A rule that withdraws a fee entitlement from a defendant who committed intentional misconduct classifies by what the defendant *did*. Florida already draws this line inside chapter 768, and the drafting precedent is exact:

> ⭐ **§ 768.735(1), verbatim:** "Sections 768.72(2)-(4), 768.725, and 768.73 do not apply to any civil action based upon child abuse, abuse of the elderly under chapter 415, or abuse of the developmentally disabled."

**The Legislature has already decided, inside part II of chapter 768, that the intentional-abuse class is exempt from three of that part's damages provisions.** Scenario B is that same decision extended one section over. A bill analysis that leads with § 768.735 is not proposing a novel classification; it is proposing consistency.

**(b) Classification by the claimant's identity — weaker, and avoidable.** ⚠️ The plurality in *Estate of McCall v. United States*, 134 So. 3d 894 (Fla. 2014), invalidated § 766.118's wrongful-death cap in part because its burden fell arbitrarily by family composition. **Nothing here should classify by who the claimant is.** Classify by the conduct sued upon, or by the defendant's conviction. Every scenario below is drafted that way.

## Gate 4 — Retroactivity, and this is the sleeper

🔴 **This gate does not appear in the carrier memo and it is the most likely thing to be missed.**

[*Metropolitan Dade County v. Jones Boatyard, Inc.*, 611 So. 2d 512 (Fla. 1993)](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1925102/metropolitan-dade-county-v-jones-boatyard-inc/) holds that the applicable version of § 768.79 is **the one in effect when the cause of action accrued** — not when the offer was served. That is because § 768.79 sits inside an integrated statutory scheme governed by § 768.71(2). By contrast § 45.061's trigger was the making of the offer (*Leapai*). Different statutes, different triggers, and this one is the unfavourable trigger.

**Why that bites harder here than anywhere else in the regime.** Intentional-tort claims have the longest tails in Florida law:

- **§ 95.11(8)** — an action founded on abuse as defined in §§ 39.01, 415.102, or 984.03, or on incest, may be commenced within **seven years after the age of majority**, or four years after leaving the abuser's dependency, or four years after discovery, whichever is later.
- **§ 95.11(10)** — an action related to a violation of § 794.011 (sexual battery), or under § 787.061, involving a victim **under 16 at the time of the act, may be commenced at any time**.

⭐ **So the claimants with the most exposure to § 768.79 are precisely the claimants a 2027 amendment would reach last.** A survivor filing in 2028 on 1990s conduct would, under *Jones Boatyard*, be governed by the 1997 version of the statute. The reform would protect the newest claims first and the oldest claims — the ones the long-tail statutes exist to preserve — not at all, or only after decades.

**Three responses, in order of preference:**

1. **Draft the applicability clause explicitly and key it to the service of the proposal**, not to accrual. State in the bill that the provision governs any proposal served on or after the effective date. ⚠️ This is a legislative override of a construction, not a violation of a holding — *Jones Boatyard* construed the scheme's default, and the Legislature may set a different trigger for a new provision, as § 45.061 shows it has done before. But expect the argument that § 768.71(2) supplies a scheme-wide rule.
2. **Frame the change as one that withdraws a defendant's entitlement rather than creating a claimant's right.** Retroactivity doctrine is at its strictest where vested rights are impaired. A defendant has no vested right in a fee award for a proposal it has not yet served.
3. **Accept a prospective-only rule for Scenario B and rely on Scenario A** — whose good-faith provision operates on the *proposal*, not on the claim — **to cover the long-tail cases.** This is a real argument for running A first.

⚠️ **No case in this project's corpus squarely addresses whether the Legislature can set a proposal-date trigger for a new § 768.79 subsection.** Treat this as the leading open research question in the memo.

# 4. Scenario A — The Nominal Offer Fix

**Framing:** "The statute was written to produce settlements. A one-dollar offer is not a settlement offer."
**Ask:** technical, side-neutral on its face, low salience.
**Audience:** House Civil Justice & Claims Subcommittee and Senate Judiciary — the committees that kill fee bills.

## What it does

**A-1. A good-faith floor.** Amend § 768.79(8)(a): in an action in which punitive damages are sought or in which the claim arises from intentional misconduct as defined in § 768.72(2)(a), a proposal is **presumed not to have been made in good faith** where it purports to resolve a punitive-damages claim for a nominal amount, or where the offeror served no written statement of the basis for the amount. Rebuttable by a preponderance.

**A-2. The punitive-gate provision.** Entitlement does not arise from a proposal served while a motion under § 768.72 is **pending and undetermined**, and the 30-day acceptance period for a proposal served before such a motion is filed does not generate entitlement if the motion is granted within it.

**A-3. Codify the comparator.** State expressly that "judgment obtained" under § 768.79(7) **includes punitive damages awarded**.

## Why each clears the gates

| | Gate 1 (art. V) | Gate 3 (EP) | Gate 4 (retroactivity) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A-1** | Good faith is a creature of the statute, at (8)(a). Legislating its standard legislates the statute's own escape clause. **Clean.** | Rational basis, trivial | Operates on the proposal, not the claim — the least exposed provision in the memo |
| **A-2** | 🔴 **The exposed one.** Must be entitlement, never timing. *Knealing* struck § 44.102(6) for exactly this error. | Trivial | Same |
| **A-3** | Defines a term in the statute's own comparison provision. **Clean** — and ⭐ *CCM Condominium* (2021) shows the Court has never examined the *White* formula on the merits, declining under *State v. Poole*. Codification settles a question the Court has twice declined to reach. | N/A | Same |

⭐ **A-1 is the highest-value-per-unit-of-risk item here**, for the same reason the carrier memo's A-1 is: it legislates into an arc with no doctrine. But note the difference in what each side is asking for. The carrier wants a **presumption of good faith** keyed to the offeror's possession of an expert opinion. The claimant wants a **presumption against good faith** keyed to a nominal number. **These are directly competing occupations of the same empty ground, and they cannot both pass.** Whichever is filed first sets the default the other must dislodge.

## Odds

| | Assessment |
|---|---|
| **Passage, standalone, 2027** | **20–30%.** Fee bills do not move alone. HB 1269 (2026), on attorney fees in insurance claims, died in Civil Justice & Claims without a hearing on March 13, 2026. |
| **Passage, attached to a criminal-justice, victims', or child-protection vehicle** | **45–55%.** A-1 and A-3 read as anti-gamesmanship housekeeping and are hard to speak against on the record. |
| **FLSC survival** | **A-1: 85–90% · A-2: 60–70% · A-3: 90%+.** A-2 carries all the art. V risk in this scenario. |
| **Value to claimants** | **Moderate to high**, and higher than it looks. A-2 alone removes the pre-*768.72* proposal, which is where the worst exposure is generated. |

# 5. Scenario B — The Intentional Misconduct Carve-Out

**Framing:** "Chapter 768 already exempts abuse cases from its damages provisions. This finishes the job."
**Ask:** structural, visible, contested.
**Audience:** leadership. Will not clear a subcommittee on its own.

## What it does

Amend the scope clause of § 768.79(1). Two variants, and the choice between them is the main decision in this memo:

**B-1 — Full exclusion.** § 768.79 does not apply to a claim for damages arising from intentional misconduct. **Neither side recovers fees.**

**B-2 — One-way.** A defendant has no entitlement under § 768.79 on such a claim; the claimant's entitlement is preserved.

⭐ **Recommend B-1.** Three reasons, and the third is decisive:

1. **It is the only version that is honestly neutral**, which is the entire political case for the bill. B-2 is a one-way fee shift, and the Legislature repealed the state's most prominent one-way fee shift — § 627.428 — in ch. 2023-15 § 11, effective March 24, 2023 (recorded at *Coates* n.2). **Asking this Legislature for a new one-way shift three years later is asking it to reverse its own signature reform.**
2. **B-1 reduces litigation**, which fits the frame the majority actually uses. *Gorka* (2010) is available in the Court's own words: the sanction was meant "to reduce litigation costs and conserve judicial resources," but "[t]he effect, however, **has been in sharp contrast to the intended outcome** because the statute and rule have seemingly increased litigation."
3. **B-1 costs claimants less than it appears.** In the fact pattern that matters — an individual perpetrator with no coverage and no assets — a claimant's fee entitlement against him is worth nothing anyway. What the claimant gives up is largely notional; what the claimant avoids is real.

## The central drafting problem

🔴 **How do you define the class without inviting every plaintiff to plead a battery count?**

This is the objection that kills Scenario B if it is not answered in the bill itself, and it is a fair objection. A carve-out keyed to what the complaint *alleges* is a carve-out any competent plaintiff's lawyer can claim by adding a count. That converts a targeted exemption into a general escape hatch from § 768.79, and it hands opponents an accurate one-sentence attack.

But keying it to a **verdict** creates the opposite problem: the parties must know, at the time a proposal is served, whether the statute applies to it. A rule that is determined only at judgment makes every proposal in every case contingent.

⭐ **The answer is the § 768.72 order, and it is available because Florida already built it.** Section 768.72(1) requires "a reasonable showing by evidence in the record or proffered by the claimant which would provide a reasonable basis for recovery" before punitive damages may be pleaded at all. That produces, mid-case, on a motion, **a judicial finding on an evidentiary record that the claim has substance** — which is precisely the screening device a scope carve-out needs, and it is already part of Florida practice, with its own body of law and its own certiorari review.

So: **the carve-out attaches when the court grants leave under § 768.72 on a claim of intentional misconduct as defined in § 768.72(2)(a).** Pleading alone does not trigger it. A judge does.

Three consequences worth naming:

- It reaches the **institutional co-defendant** where the claimant obtains a § 768.72 ruling against that defendant on the § 768.72(3) corporate standard — active participation, knowing condonation, or gross negligence contributing to the loss. That is the answer to the Part 1.3 problem, and it is narrow enough to be defensible.
- It creates a **timing seam**: proposals served before the ruling. Scenario A-2 closes it. **A and B are complements, not alternatives.**
- ⚠️ It leaves out claims where punitive damages are not sought. Some intentional-tort claimants do not plead them. **Provide an alternative trigger** — an unopposed or granted motion, or a stipulation — so the carve-out does not force a punitive claim into every case. Drafting note in [`draft-language.md`](draft-language.md) § B.

## Why it clears the gates

- **Gate 1 — the strongest position in the memo.** Scope is the substantive core of the entitlement, and the Court said in the 1992 *Timmons* Supplemental Order that this clause is "beyond this Court's control because [it is] substantive." A rule cannot restore it. See Gate 1.
- **Gate 2 — open.** Nothing is abolished.
- **Gate 3 — § 768.735(1) is the template**, verbatim in Gate 3(a). Same chapter, same part, same class of conduct.
- **Gate 4 — the real exposure.** See Gate 4. Under *Jones Boatyard* this reaches only causes accruing after the effective date unless the applicability clause is drafted around it, and the long-tail claimants are exactly the ones left out.

| | Assessment |
|---|---|
| **Passage, B-1, single session (2027)** | **25–35%.** |
| **Passage, B-1, across two sessions** | **40–50%.** Florida tort legislation habitually fails once and passes narrowed. Expect the class to be cut to enumerated offenses. |
| **Passage, B-2 (one-way)** | **10–20%.** The 2023 repeal of § 627.428 is a direct headwind. |
| **FLSC survival** | **B-1: 90%+ · B-2: 85%.** ⭐ **The constitutional odds here are the highest in either memo.** Withdrawing a statutory entitlement is the least vulnerable thing the Legislature can do to this statute. |

⭐ **Note the inversion.** In the carrier memo, the big ask carried the constitutional risk and the small ask was safe. **Here it is reversed: the constitutional risk is low across the board and the entire risk is political.** That should change how the campaign is run — the effort belongs in the committee process and the coalition, not in the constitutional briefing.

# 6. Scenario C — The Convicted Offender Bar

**Framing:** "A person convicted of a crime should not collect attorney's fees from the victim of that crime."
**Ask:** small, narrow, and close to unanswerable on the record.
**Audience:** anyone. This is the one that passes.

## What it does

Where the defendant has been **convicted of, or has pled guilty or nolo contendere to**, a criminal offense arising from the same act or transaction that is the basis of the civil claim:

- **C-1.** The defendant has **no entitlement** to costs or attorney's fees under § 768.79 as against the victim of that offense, and any proposal served by that defendant confers none.
- **C-2.** The claimant's own entitlement is **enhanced**: fees run from the date of a written presuit demand served at least 30 days before suit, on the model already enacted at § 772.11.
- **C-3.** A fee award under § 768.79 against a natural person who is the victim of an offense described in C-1 is **non-recourse** — collectible only out of the judgment. (The concession chip; see Part 7.3.)

**The trigger is a conviction.** No new fact-finding, no new litigation, no discovery burden, and a standard of proof beyond anything the civil case will produce.

## Why it is the strongest constitutional design

⭐ **It has an affirmative constitutional anchor that exists only for this class.**

Article I, § 16(b), Fla. Const. — the crime victims' rights provision adopted in 2018 — guarantees a crime victim, among other rights, "**full and timely restitution in every case and from each convicted offender**," and the provision states that these rights are **self-executing** and do not require implementing legislation.

**The argument:** a § 768.79 fee award that runs from a convicted offender against that offender's victim, and that is collectible against the victim's civil recovery, is in tension with a constitutional guarantee that the flow of money between those two people runs the other way. The Legislature is entitled to legislate to effectuate a self-executing constitutional right, and a findings section can say exactly that.

⚠️ **State the weakness plainly.** The natural reading of § 16(b) is that it addresses **criminal restitution** — the § 775.089 order in the criminal case — not civil fee awards in a separate action. **No authority located either way; this appears to be a question of first impression, and the memo does not claim more than that.** But note what the argument has to do: it does not have to *win*. Article I, § 16(b) is not a gate this bill must clear — no one is challenging the bill under it. It is a **rationale**, and under rational-basis review a legislative judgment that a statute is in tension with a constitutional guarantee of victim restitution is a more than sufficient basis for withdrawing the entitlement.

⭐ **That is the structural difference from the carrier memo's article I, § 26 point.** There, the constitutional provision was an obstacle to be mitigated. Here it is a reason to be cited. Same posture, opposite sign.

## Why it is the biggest political prize per dollar of effort

**There is no natural opposition.** The Florida Justice Reform Institute, the Chamber, and the carriers do not have a stake in preserving fee awards for convicted criminals; the defendants who benefit from the current rule are, by the terms of the provision, convicted offenders. The bill can be sponsored from either party and drafted into any criminal-justice or victims' vehicle.

**And it does the thing that matters most for the rest of the package: it creates the category.** Once § 768.79 contains a subsection that turns on "an action arising from conduct for which the defendant was convicted," Scenario B is no longer the creation of a new exemption. It is the **widening of an existing one** — from convicted conduct to judicially-screened conduct. Legislatures widen categories they have already adopted far more readily than they adopt new ones. **Scenario C is worth running even if it were worth nothing on its own, which it is not.**

## Where it is weak

🔴 **It largely misses the defendant that matters.** Per Part 1.3, the proposals in these cases come from the institutional co-defendant, which is not convicted of anything. C-1 reaches the perpetrator, against whom § 768.79 exposure was rarely the practical problem.

That is a real limitation and it should be conceded openly rather than papered over — conceding it is what makes the ask for B credible. **C is the easiest to pass and the least valuable; B is the hardest to pass and the most valuable. Value and feasibility run in opposite directions**, the same inversion the carrier memo found on its own side of the statute.

Two further points:

- ⚠️ **Timing.** Criminal cases resolve on their own schedule. A conviction may post-date the civil judgment, or never come — declination, a plea to an unrelated count, a defendant who dies or is incompetent. **Draft C-1 to attach on conviction at any time before the fee determination becomes final, and add a tolling or re-opening provision**, or the provision will miss most of the cases it is written for.
- **Nolo pleas** must be included by name. Florida practice makes a nolo plea the ordinary disposition in many of these prosecutions, and a provision limited to "convicted" invites litigation about whether adjudication was withheld.

| | Assessment |
|---|---|
| **Passage, standalone or attached, 2027** | **55–70%.** The highest in either memo. |
| **FLSC survival** | **85–90%.** Withdrawal of an entitlement, classified by criminal conviction — the most defensible classification available. |
| **Value to claimants** | **Low to moderate**, standing alone. **High as a predicate for B.** |

⭐ **Recommendation: file C first and alone. Attach A to it or to the next available vehicle. Come back for B-1 in the following session, on the record C creates.**

That ordering is not a hedge. It reflects three findings:

1. **C creates the statutory category B needs** (Part 6).
2. **A covers the long-tail claims B cannot reach**, because A operates on the proposal and B is caught by *Jones Boatyard* (Gate 4).
3. **A is a race.** The carrier side is drafting a competing occupation of § 768.79(8)(a). Arc 6 has been empty for forty years; it will not stay empty once either side moves.

## 7.2 The rulemaking flank — and the immediate action item

The Court is the more active editor of this field: eight rule amendments against three substantive statutory changes in forty years ([`../arc-matrix.md`](../arc-matrix.md)). Two moves follow, and the first is time-sensitive.

🔴 ***Trace Elements* as it stands is claimant-favorable, and the Court has invited its own reversal.**

[*Trace Elements, Inc. v. MacKensen*](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/10883903/trace-elements-inc-v-nadja-mackensen/) (Fla. July 2, 2026), 4–3, holds that a joint proposal must apportion even where the claim is unified. In intentional-tort litigation the defense is routinely **multi-defendant** — perpetrator plus institution plus, often, a staffing or management entity — so the global joint proposal is the defense's natural instrument, and *Trace Elements* invalidates it. **The decision, whatever one thinks of it doctrinally, currently operates as a shield for multi-defendant claimants.**

And the majority **invited a rule amendment**, expressly citing the Court's post-*Lamb* adoption of rule 1.442(c)(4) as its model. [`../memo.md`](../memo.md) Part VI.C documents that the Court has accepted that kind of invitation before — the 2003 → 2005 → 2010 loop took seven years and ended with the Committee's original proposal adopted.

**So:**

1. **Appear in the rules proceeding.** If the Civil Procedure Rules Committee takes up (c)(3), claimant-side comments belong in that docket. This costs a filing and nothing else.
2. **Do not seek anything by rule that could be sought by statute.** Anything won by rule can be undone by rule in a single cycle, without any constitutional confrontation. ⭐ Under *Kuhajda* and *Trace Elements*, a rule provision that does not implement a substantive statutory requirement is doubly fragile. **Every provision in this package is drafted as substantive not only to survive article V, but to be unamendable by the other institution.**

🔴 **Rehearing in *Trace Elements* was pending as of the ACIS docket printed August 3, 2026. Re-pull it before acting on this section.**

## 7.3 The concession to give up early

**C-3, the non-recourse provision**, is the cheapest concession available and it should be offered rather than defended. A fee award against a natural-person claimant, collectible only out of the judgment, removes the "this bankrupts victims" fact pattern while leaving the defense verdict case — where there is no judgment to protect — untouched.

⚠️ Note that the carrier memo independently proposes the identical mechanism for its own reasons (article I, § 26). **A provision both sides are prepared to concede is a provision that can be traded twice.** Do not spend anything to obtain it.

## 7.4 The coalition

**Already on the ground.** The Florida Justice Association maintains eight registered lobbyists and appeared on both **HB 1269** (attorney fees in insurance claims) and **HB 6003** (the wrongful-death repeal) in the 2026 session — the two bills closest to this subject matter. Registered issue areas: insurance, health, consumer protection, business and professional regulation. *(Florida lobbyist registration data, retrieved August 7, 2026.)*

**The coalition Scenario C unlocks is different from the one FJA usually assembles**, and that is the point:

- Victim-advocacy and anti-trafficking organizations
- Child-protection and elder-abuse advocates — note that § 768.735(1) already names their three constituencies
- Prosecutors and the sheriffs' association, who carry victims'-rights bills as a matter of course
- Faith-based and survivor organizations of the kind that organized around the 2025–26 wrongful-death fight

⭐ **This is a criminal-justice coalition, not a civil-justice coalition, and it does not trigger the reflexive tort-reform opposition that a bill carried by the trial bar alone will.** Sequencing C first is partly a coalition decision: it lets the package be introduced by the members who carry victims' bills rather than by the members who carry plaintiff bills.

**Opposition to expect on B:** the Florida Justice Reform Institute, the Florida Chamber, and the property and casualty carriers — who will read B correctly as an erosion of HB 837's architecture, and will oppose it on that ground rather than on its merits.

## 7.6 The empirical predicate

The findings section needs numbers, and they do not exist yet.

1. ⚠️ **The district-court corpus is untouched** — ~816 opinions, flagged at [`../memo.md`](../memo.md) Part VIII.E. That corpus is where the answer lives to the only question a committee will actually ask: **how often is § 768.79 invoked against intentional-tort claimants, and with what result?** Building it is the largest unbudgeted item here and it is separately the most valuable extension of the parent research project.
2. **Produce the metric that matters:** the frequency of nominal punitive-damages proposals, and the rate at which fee awards are entered against claimants who obtained a verdict but not a large enough one. That second number is the bill.
3. ⭐ **Use the Court's own words in the findings.** *Gorka*: the effect "has been in sharp contrast to the intended outcome." *Anderson* and *Allen v. Nunez*: the nitpicking practice has "created **more judicial labor, not less**." *Allen* (Polston, J., dissenting, with Canady, C.J., and Lawson, J.): the jurisprudence "**in this area of the law seems inconsistently applied and unpredictable**." **A findings section quoting three justices describing this regime as unpredictable is close to unanswerable under rational-basis review** — and it reframes the bill as a response to judicial diagnosis rather than a favor to the trial bar. *(The carrier memo makes the same recommendation with the same quotations. The diagnosis is genuinely shared; only the remedy is contested.)*

# 8. What claimants' counsel can do today, without a statute

None of this requires legislation, and all of it is available now.

1. ⭐ **Plead separately and watch *Diamond Aircraft*.** [*Diamond Aircraft Industries, Inc. v. Horowitch*, 107 So. 3d 362 (Fla. 2013)](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4988617/diamond-aircraft-industries-inc-v-horowitch/) holds § 768.79 inapplicable to an action seeking both damages and equitable relief, at least where the proposal is a general one resolving all claims — and **expressly reserved** whether the result changes when monetary and nonmonetary claims are pled *separately* and the proposal targets only the monetary claim. That reservation is a live drafting opportunity, and intentional-tort complaints frequently carry genuine equitable counts — injunctive relief against contact, return or destruction of images, accounting. ⚠️ It is a reserved question, not a holding; do not treat it as safe harbour.
2. **Move under § 768.72 early.** A granted motion changes the valuation posture and, under Scenario B, would become the trigger. It is worth doing on the current law regardless.
3. **Challenge the nominal punitive proposal on good-faith grounds and make a record.** Arc 6 is empty because nobody has litigated it to a decision. A well-built good-faith record in a nominal-proposal case is both a defense to the fee claim and the evidentiary predicate for A-1.
4. **Serve demands.** § 768.79 is symmetric, and a claimant who serves a supported demand and beats it by 25% recovers fees against a solvent institutional defendant. In this posture the statute is an asset.
5. **Read the proposal against rule 1.442(c)(3).** *Trace Elements* is currently good law and multi-defendant proposals are its target.

# 9. The case against

Stated plainly, because a strategy paper that does not is not worth much.

1. ⭐ **The statute is a sword too, and B-1 breaks it.** Against a solvent institutional defendant that refuses to engage, a claimant's demand plus fee entitlement is among the few pressure mechanisms available in Florida civil practice. B-1 gives that up in exactly the cases where the defendant has money. **The honest framing of B-1 is a trade: certainty against upside.** Whether that trade is right depends on data nobody has (Part 7.6) — specifically, on whether claimant-side § 768.79 recoveries in intentional-tort cases are more or less frequent than defense-side awards. **If they are more frequent, Scenario B is affirmatively bad for claimants and should be dropped.**

2. **The pleading-manipulation objection is fair and will be made.** The § 768.72-order trigger answers it, but at a cost: it makes the carve-out unavailable to claimants who do not seek punitive damages, and it puts the availability of the exemption in the hands of a trial judge on a motion with certiorari-only review.

3. **The political environment is hostile and recent.** HB 837 (2023) and the repeal of § 627.428 established that this Legislature's position is fewer fee entitlements and fewer carve-outs. **Every scenario here asks for a carve-out from a tort-reform statute, from the Legislature that wrote it.** C survives that objection because its beneficiary class is crime victims and its burden falls on convicted offenders. B does not.

4. **Retroactivity may hollow out the win.** Gate 4. The claimants with the longest exposure are the ones a prospective amendment reaches last, and the fix depends on an applicability clause whose validity is unresearched.

5. **The good-faith race may already be lost.** If a carrier-side presumption of good faith passes first, A-1 is not a fresh occupation of empty ground — it is an attempt to dislodge a recent enactment, which is a much harder ask.

6. **A statute that passes can be narrowed by construction.** The regime's history is a Court reading this statute more strictly than its text requires. The Legislature has reached into § 768.79 substantively three times in forty years and got, most recently, a carve-out for one industry in one category of action. **That is the realistic ceiling on a first attempt. Plan for the second bill before the first one passes.**
